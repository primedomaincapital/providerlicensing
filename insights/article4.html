How Long Provider Credentialing Takes — and What Each Delay Costs the Practice

Ninety days is the middle of the range, not the top of it. Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial payers each run their own clock — and each week of delay carries a price the practice has already paid.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

On a Tuesday in late September, a family practice in Ohio hired its fourth physician. She was board-certified, licensed in the state, and ready to see patients the following Monday. The practice manager filed the enrollment paperwork the same week, confident the file was clean. The first claim the practice could bill for her work went out well into the following season. In between, she saw patient after patient, and the practice paid her salary, her benefits, and her share of the rent out of cash it had already collected from other doctors’ work. Nothing about her qualifications was ever in question. The file simply moved at the speed of the slowest payer on the list.

That is the normal shape of credentialing in the United States, and the first thing to understand about it is that there is no single clock. There are three, and they run at different speeds. Medicare enrollment runs through PECOS, the federal Provider Enrollment, Chain, and Ownership System. Medicaid enrollment runs through each state, on that state’s forms, on that state’s schedule. Commercial credentialing runs payer by payer, each plan doing its own primary source verification, each with its own committee calendar. A provider joining a group with ten payer relationships runs ten clocks at once, and the group cannot bill any of them in network until each one finishes.

Medicare is the fastest clock, and still not fast

Medicare is the best of the three, which is a low bar. A clean electronic application submitted through PECOS typically processes in about 15 days for roughly 95 percent of submissions that need no site visit, according to MedBillingTech’s 2026 timeline review — but that is the enrollment decision, not the whole path, and full enrollment including primary source verification typically takes 60 to 90 days, the range MD Revenue Group and DoctorMgt both report for 2026. When a site visit or a development letter is involved, the range stretches to 45 to 65 days for the application alone, and paper applications add another 15 to 30 days on top. PayrHealth, updated in November 2026, puts clean Medicare at 30 to 60 days and warns that the effective date can be set no more than 30 days before the submission date — file too early and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services sends it back unprocessed. Roughly 40 percent of applications require corrections that add 15 to 30 days or more, according to DoctorsManagement. The fast payer is fast only when the file is perfect.

Medicaid is where the clock breaks down. There is no federal processing deadline, because there is no federal process: fifty states run fifty enrollments. MedBillingTech’s state-by-state review puts the range at 45 to 180 days. Florida and California tend to land in the 45-to-90-day band. Texas runs 60 to 120. New York is the slowest in the country, regularly hitting 90 to 180 days. PayrHealth puts Medicaid fee-for-service at 60 to 180 days and notes that some states take up to a year, while Medicaid managed care plans — Centene, Molina, Anthem, and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan among them — take another 60 to 180 days after state enrollment, because the plans will not accept an application until the provider holds a traditional Medicaid number. A practice that takes Medicaid in New York is planning around a six-month wait before the first managed-care claim can go out.

Commercial payers set the pace, and the pace is ninety days at best

Commercial credentialing is the long pole. MD Revenue Group puts commercial payers at 90 to 150 days, with some large networks stretching to 180. DoctorMgt reports the same 90-to-150-day band for the major national plans — Aetna, Cigna, UnitedHealthcare, Anthem — and cites committee schedules, backlogs, and closed panels as the usual drivers. PayrHealth puts national commercial plans at 60 to 150 days and regional Blue Cross Blue Shield plans at 90 to 180. The spread inside one payer type is the point: an audit of 1,000 credentialing applications by nCred, reported by DoctorMgt, found an average of 64 days, with the fastest file clearing in 21 days and the slowest taking 201. Two providers at the same practice, credentialed with the same payers, can be billable three months apart for no reason either of them controls.

The bill for the wait is measured in revenue that cannot be sent, not in fees. The Medical Group Management Association’s figures, reported by Becker’s Hospital Review in August 2025, put credentialing and payer enrollment delays at about $10,122 per provider, per month, in deferred revenue and access limitations. The MGMA’s own surveys put the scale in human terms: 67 percent of medical groups report onboarding delays of 60 to 90 days, and 72 percent report revenue loss tied directly to enrollment delays. A Merritt Hawkins survey of hospital chief financial officers found the average physician generates roughly $2.38 million in net revenue a year for an affiliated organization — about $6,500 a day. At that rate, a ninety-day wait on one physician holds back close to $590,000 in revenue that the practice has already spent money to earn. Three hires a year, each waiting ninety days, is the cost of a small building in unbillable time.

Where the weeks actually go

Almost none of the delay is the verification itself. Primary source verification — confirming the degree, the license, the sanctions record — is a check against a database, and it takes days. The weeks go to the queue, the committee, and the contract. CAQH, the Council for Affordable Quality Healthcare, runs the shared data portal where more than 2.5 million providers maintain profiles that more than 1,000 health plans pull from, and the profile must be re-attested every 120 days or payers lose access. A lapsed attestation freezes every pending application at once. After approval, contract execution and loading take another two to six weeks before the first claim can go out, according to DoctorsManagement. The National Committee for Quality Assurance requires most verifications to be no more than 180 calendar days old when the credentialing decision is made, so a file that sits in a queue too long can age out of its own verifications and need them done again. The system does not just move slowly. It can move slowly enough to restart itself.

The honest planning number is the top of the range, not the middle. Start 120 days before the intended start date, every 2026 guide agrees, and treat the top of each payer’s range as the plan: 90 days for Medicare, 180 for Medicaid in a slow state, 150 to 180 for commercial. Starting at 60 days nearly guarantees a billing gap. That is not pessimism. It is what the nCred audit shows: a 21-day file and a 201-day file are the same process, and the difference between them is which queue the file landed in and whether anyone followed up while it sat there. The practices that bill on time are not the ones with cleaner files. They are the ones that file early and chase the file every week.

The counterargument: the queue is doing real work

It is worth saying plainly that most of this machinery exists for a reason. Credentialing catches fraud, discipline, and expired credentials that a self-reported form would miss, and the payers that move slowly are in part the payers that check carefully. Primary source verification through the National Practitioner Data Bank and federal exclusion lists is the difference between a licensed physician and a convincing résumé. A system that approved every file in fifteen days by checking less would be faster and worse. The counterargument has a limit, though, and Tampa General Hospital found it: the 982-bed academic medical center cut credentialing turnaround from 30 days to a median of 3 days in its first six months working with Medallion, with an estimated $2.4 million return on investment, and nothing about the verification changed. The queue changed. Speed and rigor are not the same trade-off the wait implies.

What that means for a practice is blunt. The credentialing clock is not a single delay to be endured but a set of payer clocks to be managed separately, each with its own top-of-range plan, its own attestation deadline, and its own follow-up owner. The revenue at stake — $10,122 a month per provider by the MGMA’s count, $2.38 million a year per physician by Merritt Hawkins’ — is large enough that the file deserves the same attention as the hire itself. The physician’s first day is not the start of the work. The paperwork filed four months earlier is.
