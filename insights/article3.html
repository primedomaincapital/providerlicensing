What ProviderLicensing.com Is Worth

HealthInsurance.com sold for $8.13 million. Medicare.com sold for $4.8 million. This name is priced at $9,888 — and the arithmetic behind that price is the point.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

The email arrived on a Tuesday. A credentialing software company had just closed a funding round, and its chief executive was looking for a name that said what the company did before the first sentence of the pitch deck. The broker's list had three names on it. Two were invented words, the kind that sound like a startup and mean nothing to anyone outside the room. The third was ProviderLicensing.com. It was the only name on the list that a hospital chief operating officer would understand without an explanation, and it was the only one that cost less than a single month of one physician's delayed billing.

That is the arithmetic this name rests on, and it is worth doing in full. The Medical Group Management Association puts credentialing and payer enrollment delays at about $10,122 per provider, per month, in deferred revenue. The Buy Now price of ProviderLicensing.com is $9,888. One month of delay on one provider costs more than the domain. A Merritt Hawkins survey of hospital chief financial officers found the average physician generates roughly $2.38 million in net revenue a year for an affiliated organization — about $198,000 a month. Against that, $9,888 is five percent of one month. Outsourced credentialing runs $100 to $300 per payer per provider, and a full initial package commonly runs $2,500 to $5,000; a practice credentialing four providers with one vendor has already spent more than this domain costs. The price is not a stretch. The price is a rounding error inside the problem the name describes.

The comparable sales set the ceiling, and the ceiling is high. HealthInsurance.com sold for $8,130,000 in 2019, the highest reported health .com sale on record. Medicare.com sold for $4,800,000 in 2014 to eHealthInsurance. Pharmacy.com sold for $2,300,000 in 2008. Loans.com sold for $3,000,000 in 2000 to Bank of America. These are category names, not like-for-like comparisons — a two-word exact match in a regulated category is worth more than a two-word exact match in an unregulated one, because the regulated category has fewer names and richer buyers. But they establish the shape of the market: health and finance category names trade in seven and eight figures when the buyer is a company, not a speculator. ProviderLicensing.com is priced at 0.12 percent of HealthInsurance.com's price and 0.21 percent of Medicare.com's price. The gap between the ceiling and this price is the opportunity, and it is also the risk: the gap exists because no provider-licensing name has sold publicly at a high price yet, and the first sale in a category is always the one that has to prove the category exists.

The buyer pool is the strongest part of the case. Medallion has raised about $130 million at an estimated valuation of about $440 million, with contracted annual revenue approaching $50 million. Verifiable has raised about $47 million. CertifyOS has raised about $69 million. symplr was acquired by Clearlake Capital in 2018 at a $550 million valuation, with a hoped-for $2 billion sale price discussed in 2020, and has since made eleven acquisitions. Modio Health was acquired by CHG Healthcare, the largest physician staffing company in the country. These are not hypothetical buyers. They are funded companies whose entire business is the two words in this domain, and each of them currently operates under a name that is either invented (Medallion, Verifiable, CertifyOS), an acronym-adjacent brand (symplr), or a staffing company's subsidiary (Modio). None of them owns the category name. The first one that does will own the search result, the email address, and the conference badge that every hospital administrator reads first.

Beyond the software companies sits a second pool. Credentialing outsourcers charge $100 to $300 per payer application and $2,500 to $5,000 for a full initial package, and they sell trust to practices that cannot staff a credentialing department. Telehealth groups license clinicians across dozens of states at once — the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact counted more than 49,000 physicians and more than 170,000 licenses issued as of its FY2025 report, with an average of 4.0 licenses per applicant — and each of those licenses is a file that has to be managed. Revenue cycle management firms, which already sell into the same chief financial officers, add licensing as a service line. Every one of these buyers measures the domain against the same yardstick: one month of one provider's delay, $10,122, against a one-time price of $9,888. The domain pays for itself the first time it shortens a single credentialing cycle by a single month, if it shortens it at all.

The counterargument is the honest one, and it should be stated plainly. No provider-licensing .com has sold publicly for a high price, so there is no direct comparable. The median reported .com sale in 2024–2026 was about $4,953, and this name is priced at roughly twice that median — a premium that has to be justified by the buyer pool rather than by a precedent. The category's venture funding is a bet, not a guarantee: valuations are estimates, none of these companies is public, and a downturn in digital health funding would shrink the pool of buyers who can pay five figures for a name without a board discussion. A buyer could also choose a cheaper invented name and spend the difference on advertising, and many successful software companies have done exactly that — Medallion itself is an invented name, and it has raised $130 million under it.

That argument is strongest on price and weakest on the buyer. An invented name works when the company behind it spends years and millions of dollars teaching the market what it means. An exact-match name works on the first day, before the first dollar of advertising, because the customer already knows the words — a hospital administrator searching for provider licensing is not searching for Medallion. She is searching for the phrase. The domain's value is not the traffic it receives. It is the traffic it does not have to buy: every search, every referral, every conference conversation that starts with the words the buyer already uses. At $9,888, the price is one month of one provider's waiting, set against a name that works for as long as the company owns it. The companies in this market have already spent hundreds of millions of dollars to own the work. The name that describes the work is the cheapest part of the whole enterprise.
