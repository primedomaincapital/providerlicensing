What a Medical License Costs, State by State

A Pennsylvania license costs $35. A Nevada license costs $1,425. A physician licensed in five states pays more than $3,000 in application fees before seeing a single patient.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday in April, from the Medical Board of California, and it contained a bill for $1,151. The physician who opened it had already paid $695 for Step 1 of the licensing exam, $695 for Step 2, and $955 for Step 3 — $2,345 before she had ever applied for a license anywhere. The California fee was the most expensive single line on the page, and it was only the first state. She wanted to practice in five. By the time the fifth board had cashed its check, the application fees alone had passed $3,000, and nobody had yet asked her to see a patient.

That is the ordinary arithmetic of an American medical license: there is no national license, there are fifty state boards, and each one sets its own price. The Federation of State Medical Boards publishes the table, and the spread in its 2024 edition runs from $35 for a Pennsylvania MD license to $1,425 for a Nevada MD license — a forty-fold gap for the same permission to practice medicine. Missouri sits at $75 in the FSMB table. California charges $1,151 for an MD license. Rhode Island charges $1,090. Texas charges $817. New York charges $735. Florida charges $424 for an MD license and $305 for a DO license. The fee is set by the board, it is almost always non-refundable, and it buys nothing but the review of the application.

The five-state bill, line by line

Take the five states a telehealth physician is most likely to want first — California, Texas, New York, Florida, and Pennsylvania — and add the FSMB application fees: $1,151 for California, $817 for Texas, $735 for New York, $424 for Florida, and $35 for Pennsylvania. The total is $3,162 in application fees alone. That is before the Federation Credentials Verification Service, the FSMB’s primary-source verification profile, which costs $395 for the initial application including one profile sent to a designated board, with subsequent profiles at $99 each — $65 each when ordered at the same time as the initial application. Add the FCVS profile and four subsequent distributions and the five-state bill reaches roughly $3,800, before background checks, transcript fees, and the postage of a career’s worth of documents. The exams that made the physician eligible in the first place cost another $2,345: Step 1 at $695 and Step 2 CK at $695, with Step 3 at $955, the USMLE’s published fees for 2026 and 2027, the same for U.S. and international graduates. A physician licensed in five states has spent more than $6,000 on permission before earning a dollar in any of them.

The spread is not random. Nevada’s $1,425 covers the application, the registration, and the criminal background investigation in one charge, and the state renews every two years at $750. Rhode Island’s $1,090 renews at $1,090 every two years — the renewal costs as much as the first license. California’s $1,151 renews at $1,151 every two years. At the other end, Pennsylvania’s $35 application renews at $360, and Missouri’s $75 application renews at $100 in the FSMB table, though Missouri’s own current regulation lists a $102 permanent licensure fee — a reminder that the FSMB table is a snapshot and boards change their fees. Texas charges $817 to apply and then $270 to $456 for initial registration, renewing every two years. New York charges $735 to apply and $600 to renew every two years. The cheap states are cheap once. The expensive states are expensive forever.

The compact changes the speed, not the price

The Interstate Medical Licensure Compact is the one structural answer to the five-state bill, and it is honest about what it does and does not do. The Compact charges a $700 application fee on top of each state’s own license fee — $700 plus the state fee, every time, non-refundable — and in exchange it runs one qualification review, one background check, and one letter of qualification that participating states accept instead of starting from scratch. As of February 2026 the Compact counted 44 member jurisdictions — 42 states, the District of Columbia, and Guam — with more than 180,000 licenses issued and more than 54,000 physicians using the process, an average of 4.0 licenses per applicant. By June 2026 the American Medical Association reported more than 200,000 licenses issued through the Compact, with 3,633 licenses issued in March 2026 alone. The Compact’s own fee table shows the state fees inside it running from $35 in Pennsylvania to $895 in Texas.

Run the five-state example through the Compact and the catch appears at once: California and New York are not members. Neither are Massachusetts, Oregon, South Carolina, or Virginia. A physician who wants California and New York — the two largest markets in the country — applies to those boards directly, at $1,151 and $735, and the Compact helps with the other three. Add the $700 Compact fee to Texas at $895 through the Compact, Florida, and Pennsylvania, and the three-state Compact bill is roughly $1,700 to $2,700 all-in, according to Eureka Health’s July 2026 guide — on top of the $1,886 for California and New York directly. The Compact saves months. It does not save dollars. For a single additional state, the $700 fee on top of the state fee makes the direct application cheaper; the Compact pays for itself only when the physician wants several states at once, which is exactly the telehealth case.

The renewals are the real bill

The application fee is the smallest part of the lifetime cost, because licenses renew. Most states renew every two years — California, Nevada, Rhode Island, Florida, Texas, New York — and a few renew every year, including Missouri and several others in the FSMB table. A physician holding five licenses pays five renewals on five different schedules: California $1,151, New York $600, Texas $270 to $456, Florida $360, Pennsylvania $360. That is roughly $2,800 every two years to keep the same five licenses, before any continuing education, before any board certification maintenance, and before the FCVS profile updates that some boards require. Over a thirty-year career, the renewals dwarf the application fees by an order of magnitude. The $3,162 five-state application bill is a rounding error against $40,000 or more in renewals for the same five states.

There is a counterargument, and it is the one the boards themselves make: the fee pays for the verification that keeps unqualified practitioners out of the exam room, and a board that charges $35 cannot fund the same investigation as a board that charges $1,425. Pennsylvania’s $35 application fee is real, and so is the fact that Pennsylvania’s board still has to verify the degree, the training, and the disciplinary record — the work does not get cheaper because the fee is lower. The gap between $35 and $1,425 is not a gap in the work. It is a gap in how states choose to fund it, and in how much of the cost they push onto the applicant versus the taxpayer. The physician pays either way, in the fee or in the wait while an underfunded board works through its queue.

What that means in practice is that a physician choosing where to license is choosing a cost structure, not just a map. The five-state bill — $3,162 in applications, about $3,800 with verification, more than $6,000 with the exams — is the price of the first year. The renewals are the price of every year after. The Compact cuts the waiting time for the states it covers and adds $700 to the bill, and it does not cover the two states most physicians want most. Anyone budgeting a multi-state practice should price the renewals first, the applications second, and the Compact fee last — because the renewals are the bill that never stops arriving.
