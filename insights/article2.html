The Credentialing Software Race

Medallion, Verifiable, CertifyOS, and symplr have raised hundreds of millions to automate the same paperwork. The winner may be the one that makes the paperwork disappear.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

Derek Lo started Medallion in 2020 with a plain observation: the paperwork that decides whether a doctor can work had become a product category. He had watched health systems pay people to chase licenses, degrees, and payer forms by hand, and he thought software could do the chasing. Six years later, the company he founded has raised about $130 million, carries an estimated valuation of about $440 million, and reports contracted annual revenue approaching $50 million. Its customers include Tampa General Hospital, a 982-bed academic medical center in Florida, and digital health firms such as Headspace. In September 2026 it acquired Andros, a credentialing verification organization with more than one million providers across nearly 400 healthcare organizations and health plans, to form what it calls the largest AI-native credentialing platform in the country.

Medallion is not alone. It is the most visible company in a category that now has a full field. Verifiable, founded in Austin in 2019 by Nick Macario, raised a $27 million Series B in July 2023 led by Craft Ventures, bringing its total raised to about $47 million, and counts Humana, Talkspace, Modern Health, and Headway among its customers. CertifyOS, founded in 2020 by Anshul Rathi, raised a $14.5 million Series A in 2022 led by General Catalyst and a $40 million Series B in June 2025 led by Transformation Capital, bringing its total to about $69 million, and became an NCQA-accredited credentials verification organization. symplr, founded in 2006 and based in Houston, is the incumbent: Clearlake Capital acquired it in 2018 at a $550 million valuation, considered selling it in 2020 at a hoped-for $2 billion valuation, and has since funded eleven acquisitions, including a $340 million purchase of Conduent's Midas solutions in January 2022. It says it is trusted in 9 of 10 United States hospitals and by more than 400 health plans. Modio Health, founded in San Francisco in 2014, was acquired by CHG Healthcare in October 2019 — the category's early exit, and the proof that a credentialing platform could be bought by a staffing giant.

The money follows a simple count. Lo puts the number of credentialed providers in the United States at about 4 million, each contracted with an average of 19 payers. That is roughly 25 million credentialing events a year, by his calculation, each one a file that has to be assembled, verified, submitted, and watched. CertifyOS puts the annual cost of verifying provider credentials in the United States at more than $2 billion and claims as much as 75 percent of it is avoidable — its own figure, stated in its own press release, and worth treating as a claim rather than a measurement. The company also says providers file an average of 17 applications a year, worth roughly $700 in time and effort, and that plans pay $200 to $500 per provider per recredentialing cycle. The figures differ in detail. They agree in shape: the work is repetitive, it is counted in the millions, and it is done mostly by hand.

What the companies sell differs in where they sit. Medallion sells a platform with a fee plus bundles for licensing, payer enrollment, and credentialing, and in 2025 it launched CredAlliance, a clearinghouse in which payers share credentialing data — five of the ten largest health plans were considering joining at launch, Lo said. Verifiable sells an API-first verification layer that shortens credentialing from weeks to days, built inside the Salesforce ecosystem. CertifyOS sells provider data infrastructure through an API, tracking more than 1,600 data points on a single provider and managing more than 15 million medical licenses, and in September 2025 it launched a National Shared Credentialing Program with UnitedHealthcare, Cigna Healthcare, and Centene as its first participating plans, routing recredentialing rosters through a single outside platform. symplr sells the broadest platform of all — provider data management, workforce management, quality, and spend — assembled through acquisition, with credentialing as one module inside an enterprise operations suite. Modio sold credentialing and career management to healthcare organizations before its acquisition, and now sits inside the largest physician staffing company in the country.

The shared-credentialing idea is the one to watch, because it is the only idea that attacks the duplication itself. If a provider is verified once and the verification is accepted by every payer, the 19-portal problem collapses. CertifyOS says 1.4 million to 2 million providers will complete credentialing through its shared program next year. Medallion's CredAlliance rests on the same logic. The difficulty is not technical. It is that each payer is responsible for its own network, and delegating verification means trusting someone else's check. The NCQA accreditation that both Medallion's Andros acquisition and CertifyOS carry exists precisely to make that trust transferable — a certified credentials verification organization has been audited against a standard the payers accept. The race, in that light, is not only about who builds the best software. It is about who becomes the standard the payers will delegate to.

The counterargument is that the category is crowded, the incumbents are entrenched, and the free alternative already exists. CAQH's provider data portal is free for providers, used by more than 1,000 health plans, and holds profiles for more than 2.5 million providers. symplr already sits inside 9 of 10 hospitals. A new platform has to displace a portal that costs nothing and an incumbent that is already installed, and the history of healthcare software is full of better products that lost to installed ones. Consolidation is already under way — Medallion buying Andros, CHG buying Modio — and consolidation usually means fewer buyers for a domain, not more. The venture funding that looks like validation can also be read as a bet that has not yet paid off: none of these companies is public, and the valuations are estimates, not prices anyone has paid in an open market.

That caution is fair, and it understates how the market actually buys. Health systems do not replace symplr with a startup in a single decision. They add a startup for the new telehealth line, or the new state, or the new provider group, and the startup grows inside the account. Tampa General did not replace its credentialing department; it cut its turnaround from 30 days to a median of 3 days and recorded an estimated $2.4 million in return in six months. The buyers in this category are not choosing a vendor for life. They are choosing a name for the front door — the name a chief operating officer types when the new hire cannot bill, and the name a telehealth group puts on the page that promises a clinician licensed in forty states. The companies that win that search will be the ones whose name says what they do before the first sentence. That is why the category's funding matters to a domain: the money is being spent to own two words, and two words are for sale.
