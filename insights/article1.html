The Ninety-Day Wait

A new physician starts work on a Monday. The first claim the practice can bill for her work may not go out for three months. The wait is the business.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

She signed the offer in March, gave notice at her old group in April, and walked into the new clinic on a Monday morning in June with a full schedule. By Friday she had seen forty patients. By the end of the month she had seen more than a hundred. The practice paid her salary, paid her malpractice premium, and paid the rent on the exam room she worked in. What it could not do, for three months, was send a bill for any of it. Her license was current. Her training was verified. Her name was simply not yet loaded into the payers' systems, and until it was, every visit she performed was work the practice had already paid for and could not yet collect on.

That is the ordinary shape of provider licensing in the United States. It is not a single form. It is three separate gates, each with its own clock. The state board licenses the clinician to practice. The hospital or health plan credentials her, checking education, training, work history, and malpractice record against primary sources. Each payer then enrolls her, verifies the same material again in its own way, and assigns an effective date. A credentialed provider still cannot bill until enrollment and contract loading are finished, and the three gates do not run in parallel by default — they run in sequence, because each one wants the output of the one before it.

The clocks are long. A clean commercial file takes 90 to 120 days from submission to an effective date, and the Medical Group Management Association puts the full range at 90 to 180 days per payer, with the American Medical Association warning it can run to 180 days. Medicare enrollment through PECOS typically runs 60 to 90 days for a clean electronic application, and paper applications run substantially longer, commonly 90 to 120 days, with roughly 40 percent of applications requiring corrections that add 15 to 30 days or more. Medicaid varies by state, commonly 60 to 120 days, and some states require Medicare enrollment first. Hospital privileging adds another 60 to 120 days, governed in part by when the credentialing committee next meets. Start 120 days before the intended start date, the guides say. Start at 60 days and a billing gap is nearly guaranteed — and 67 percent of medical groups report onboarding delays of 60 to 90 days, with 72 percent reporting revenue loss tied directly to enrollment delays.

The cost of the gap is not the filing fee. Outsourced credentialing runs $100 to $300 per payer per provider, and a full initial package commonly runs $2,500 to $5,000 per provider. The cost is the revenue that cannot be billed while the file sits in a queue. The Medical Group Management Association puts credentialing and payer enrollment delays at about $10,122 per provider, per month, in deferred revenue and access limitations. A Merritt Hawkins survey of hospital chief financial officers found the average physician generates roughly $2.38 million in net revenue a year for an affiliated organization. At that rate, a single day of delay is worth thousands of dollars, and a ninety-day wait on one physician approaches seven figures of deferred revenue. A practice that hires three physicians a year and waits ninety days on each one is carrying the cost of a small building in unbillable time.

The machinery underneath the wait is older than most of the people running it. CAQH, the Council for Affordable Quality Healthcare, runs the shared data portal — long known as CAQH ProView, rebranded as DataSpring in June 2026 — where a provider enters professional information once and authorizes health plans to pull it instead of completing a separate application for each one. More than 1,000 health plans and other organizations ask their network providers to use it, and more than 2.5 million providers maintain profiles there. The profile is free for the provider. It must be re-attested every 120 days, or it expires and payers lose access. In a CAQH survey of 1,240 physician practices in September 2019, the average practice held 20.2 health plan contracts — rural practices averaged 26, urban practices 18 — and practices that used multiple methods to exchange credentialing information spent $2,068 a month on that work, against $1,249.86 for practices that used one platform, a difference of $818.14 a month, or $9,818 a year. The portal was supposed to end the duplication. It reduced it. It did not end it, because a profile is a data source, not a decision: each plan still performs its own primary source verification and makes its own decision.

The rules keep the cycle turning. The National Committee for Quality Assurance requires most credentialing verifications to be no more than 180 calendar days old when the credentialing decision is made, a standard in force since July 1, 2025, and requires recredentialing every 36 months with no grace period. Every three years, the whole file is rebuilt. Every 120 days, the CAQH profile is re-attested. A missed re-attestation can put claims in denial within days, and a missed revalidation deadline can cost a primary care physician seeing 20 patients a day at $100 a visit roughly $2,000 a day from a lapse with a single carrier. The work never ends; it recurs on a schedule set by other people's calendars.

There is a partial escape hatch for physicians who work across state lines. The Interstate Medical Licensure Compact, an agreement among participating states, gives qualified physicians an expedited route to licenses in multiple states. As of October 31, 2026, the Compact counted 44 member jurisdictions — 42 states, the District of Columbia, and Guam — with more than 49,000 physicians using the process and more than 170,000 licenses issued. The Compact's Year 9 data study, covering applications completed between April 1, 2025 and March 31, 2026, recorded an average of 4.0 licenses per applicant. Around 80 percent of physicians in the United States qualify for licensure through the Compact. It shortens the licensing leg. It does not shorten credentialing or payer enrollment, which still run payer by payer, and California, Massachusetts, New York, Oregon, South Carolina, and Virginia remain outside it.

The counterargument is that the wait is the point. Credentialing exists because patients need to know the person treating them is who she says she is, trained where she says she was trained, and licensed where she says she is licensed. Primary source verification catches fraud, discipline, and expired credentials that a self-reported form would miss. A faster system that verified less would be a worse system, and the payers that move slowly are, in part, the payers that check carefully. The Compact itself proves the point: it is fast precisely because it trusts the home state's verification, and it works only for physicians who already hold a full, unrestricted license with no disciplinary history. Speed without verification is not an improvement; it is a different risk.

That argument holds, and it misses the shape of the waste. The verification that matters — the license, the degree, the sanctions check — is done once by a primary source and then done again, and again, by every payer that pulls the same file. The delay is not the check. The delay is the queue, the re-keying, the committee calendar, and the contract loading that follows approval by two to six weeks. Tampa General Hospital, a 982-bed academic medical center in Florida, cut its credentialing turnaround from 30 days to a median of 3 days in its first six months working with Medallion, with an estimated $2.4 million in return on investment. Nothing about the verification changed. The queue changed. That is the whole case for the companies in this market, and for the name that describes what they do: the bottleneck is not safety. The bottleneck is administration, and administration is a market.
